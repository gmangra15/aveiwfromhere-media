Pakistan's army chief in Tehran as US-Iran talks stall

Field Marshal Asim Munir held talks with Iran's top leadership on reviving negotiations with Washington. Tehran says the US must move first.

By Maps and Musings · 22 October 2026

Pakistan is once again trying to bring the United States and Iran back to the negotiating table. On Tuesday, Field Marshal Asim Munir, Pakistan's Chief of Defence Forces and army chief, travelled to Tehran, accompanied by Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi.

Who he met

In Tehran, the Pakistani delegation met President Masoud Pezeshkian, Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, and Mohsen Rezaei, the supreme leader's representative and secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council.

According to Dawn, the purpose of the visit was to convey messages and to test whether the "Islamabad understanding" could still serve as a basis for talks.

What Iran said

Iranian leaders told the Pakistani side that Washington must take the first steps to rebuild trust. They said that acceptance of Tehran's seven-point proposal could be a starting point for renewed negotiations, and that Iran would not make concessions under pressure.

Speaker Ghalibaf said the United States could not regain Iran's trust without meeting the seven conditions Tehran presented in New York, adding that "threats and military manoeuvres cannot influence the strategic decisions of the Islamic Republic of Iran," according to an Iranian account. Rezaei said Iran would not give up its enriched nuclear material.

No breakthrough, for now

No breakthrough or formal agreement was announced, and neither Pakistan's Foreign Office nor the military's media wing had commented officially at the time of reporting.

Why Pakistan matters here

Pakistan has played this role before. It helped broker a ceasefire in April, hosted direct talks between US and Iranian officials, and helped produce the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding in June, before that process stalled. Islamabad says it is still offering its good offices to both sides.

For Pakistan, the stakes are close to home: Iran is a neighbour with a long shared border and important trade links. During the visit, Munir also said a recent closure of border trade crossings was temporary and technical, and that the crossings had reopened.

Whether Washington responds to Tehran's conditions will decide if this round of shuttle diplomacy leads anywhere.
