Hingol: Pakistan's biggest national park, where mud volcanoes meet the sea

On the Makran coast of Balochistan, Hingol National Park combines desert mountains, a river estuary, sea turtles, mud volcanoes and one of South Asia's most important pilgrimage sites.

By Maps and Musings · 24 October 2026

About 190 kilometres west of Karachi, the road along Balochistan's Makran coast enters a landscape unlike anywhere else in Pakistan. This is Hingol National Park, the country's largest national park.

A park the size of a province's district

Hingol covers about 6,190 square kilometres, according to Pakistan's submission to UNESCO, spread across parts of Lasbela, Awaran and Gwadar districts. It was declared a reserved area in 1988. Its landscape runs from the sea floor and beaches of the Arabian Sea, through the estuary of the Hingol River, up to the Dhrun mountains, which reach 1,580 metres. Much of it is coastal semi-desert of drift sand, mud rock, deep gullies, cliffs and caves.

The Makran Coastal Highway, completed in 2004, runs for about 109 kilometres through the park, according to Gulf News, and is the usual way in.

Rocks, mud and sea

The wind-carved mud rock has produced famous formations such as the Princess of Hope and the Balochistan Sphinx. Inland, there are about ten clusters of mud volcanoes, which push out mud and methane rather than lava. The best known, Chandragup, is climbed by pilgrims who consider it sacred. UNESCO's listing links the volcanoes to the area's position near the meeting point of the Eurasian, Arabian and Indian tectonic plates.

Wildlife

Gulf News lists ibex, urial and chinkara gazelle in the mountains, marsh crocodiles in the river, and olive ridley and green turtles on the coast, along with around 185 bird species and 250 plant species.

Hinglaj: a shared sacred place

In a cave on a stream feeding the Hingol River lies Hinglaj Mata, one of the most important Hindu pilgrimage sites, regarded as a Shakti Peetha. According to UNESCO, more than 5,000 pilgrims gather each April and thousands more visit through the year. Local Muslims also revere the shrine, calling it Nani Mandar, and act as its custodians. Pakistan placed the "Hingol Cultural Landscape" on UNESCO's World Heritage tentative list in 2016.

Visiting

Kund Malir beach, on the park's coast, is about a three-and-a-half-hour drive from Karachi. Facilities are limited, so carry water and fuel, and check local conditions before travelling.
